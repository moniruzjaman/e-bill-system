Full Warehouses, Empty Fields: A Paradox on Paper

Bangladesh is facing an uncomfortable paradox in fertiliser management: the country says it has enough fertiliser, yet farmers in different parts of the country are struggling to obtain it when they need it.

Government figures indicate that national fertiliser stocks are substantially above projected requirements through the coming months. On paper, therefore, Bangladesh does not appear to be facing a conventional national shortage. But reports from the field tell a different story. Farmers have been standing in queues, receiving restricted quantities, travelling from one dealer to another and, in some places, paying considerably more than government-fixed prices.

This contradiction deserves closer attention. If fertiliser exists in government warehouses and the national stock position is comfortable, why is a farmer unable to obtain a bag at the official price?

The answer may lie in a distinction that is often overlooked in discussions of food and fertiliser security: national availability is not the same as farmer-level availability.

“A fertiliser bag sitting in a warehouse is of little value to a farmer whose crop needs that fertiliser today.”— The core distinction of this report

When the System Breaks: Reports from the Field

Recent reports suggest that the immediate problem is largely one of distribution and market functioning. The transition to a new fertiliser dealer policy has created uncertainty, vacancies and disruptions in the dealer network. In some areas, dealers have reportedly reduced lifting from government supply points, while farmers have encountered rationing and inadequate supplies at retail level.

The timing makes the problem particularly serious. Fertiliser demand is not evenly distributed throughout the year. It rises sharply with crop calendars and varies significantly by crop, location and production system. A national stock figure therefore cannot by itself guarantee local availability.

The farmer’s question

“Can I get the required fertiliser, in the required quantity, at the government-fixed price, from an authorised seller, at the time my crop requires it?”

Recent reports from Kurigram illustrate the consequences when that system breaks down. Farmers in Bhurungamari and other areas reportedly struggled to obtain fertiliser, while prices in unofficial markets rose substantially above official rates. In August, hundreds of farmers reportedly broke open a dealer warehouse in Bhurungamari amid frustration over supply.

Similar complaints have emerged from other districts, including parts of Rangpur, Dinajpur, Rajshahi, Bogura and Lalmonirhat. Reports have described fertiliser being rationed or unavailable through authorised channels while it remained obtainable in informal markets at higher prices.

Allegations Serious Enough to Demand Investigation

These reports should not be interpreted to mean that every dealer is responsible for wrongdoing. Nor should allegations of hoarding or artificial shortages be treated as established facts without investigation.

But the allegations themselves are serious enough to demand transparent investigation.

What is established

  • National stocks are substantially above projected requirements through the coming months.
  • The new dealer-policy transition has created uncertainty, vacancies and network disruption.
  • Farmers in multiple districts faced rationing, shortages and above-official prices.
  • State factories produced far below national demand in FY2025–26 amid gas shortages and ageing plants.

What is alleged — unproven

  • Special Branch report (as reported by The Daily Star): collusion between some dealers and retailers to create artificial shortages through hoarding and supply control.
  • Bangladesh Fertilizer Association: warnings of attempts to create an artificial crisis through hoarding and inflated prices.
These are attributed allegations, not findings. They should not automatically be applied to the entire dealer network — but they are serious enough to demand transparent, evidence-based investigation.

The government therefore faces two separate tasks. First, it must ensure that legitimate farmers receive fertiliser without disruption. Second, it must determine whether any individuals or networks are manipulating the distribution system for profit. The two tasks should not be confused.

“Blanket accusations can damage honest dealers; inadequate investigation can damage farmers.”— On evidence-based accountability

The Deeper Vulnerability: Exposed at Six Points at Once

The present disruption, however, should not obscure a much larger structural issue.

Bangladesh remains heavily dependent on imported fertiliser. More than two-thirds of annual fertiliser demand is met through imports, while domestic production has remained constrained. In fiscal year 2025–26, the country’s state-owned fertiliser factories reportedly produced only around 1.06 million tonnes against national demand of roughly 6.6 million tonnes. Several plants faced shutdowns or interruptions associated with gas shortages, ageing machinery and raw-material constraints.

What Other Countries Can Teach Us

Other countries have approached parts of this challenge differently. The lesson for Bangladesh is not to copy another country’s system wholesale, but to recognise what each approach fixes.

The visibility test: if 100 tonnes are allocated to an area, policymakers should be able to see how much actually reached dealers, how much remains in their possession, and how much has been sold to farmers.
Monitoring must follow the full chain — not stop at the national warehouse report
01
Stabilise the current market with near-real-time visibility

The government needs a transparent, near-real-time picture of fertiliser movement — not merely the amount stored nationally. Monitoring should follow the chain from national stock to regional and district allocations, upazila-level supply, dealer lifting, dealer stock and farmer sales.

Such visibility would make it much easier to distinguish genuine logistical shortages from allocation problems, delayed lifting, hoarding or diversion.

02
Make the dealer system accountable — and fair

Dealer appointments and transitions should be completed without leaving significant gaps during critical crop seasons. Dealers should have clearly defined responsibilities regarding lifting, stocking and selling fertiliser at official prices. Records should be auditable, and violations should result in proportionate enforcement.

At the same time, honest dealers should not be treated as suspects simply because a local shortage occurs. The objective should be evidence-based accountability, not indiscriminate punishment.

03
Rethink how subsidies reach farmers

A more farmer-centred system could eventually combine authorised dealers with digital records, farmer identification, transparent allocations and electronic transaction monitoring. The purpose should not be technology for its own sake. The purpose should be to make the subsidy and supply chain visible enough that fertiliser reaches the farmer for whom it is intended.

04
Rebuild domestic production capacity

Rehabilitating economically viable fertiliser plants, ensuring reliable gas supply, modernising ageing facilities and improving production efficiency should be part of a long-term fertiliser security strategy. Bangladesh should also diversify import sources and strengthen storage and logistics so that temporary disruptions abroad do not immediately translate into pressure at farm level.

05
Pursue fertiliser-use efficiency

The objective cannot simply be to make more fertiliser available and encourage higher consumption. Balanced fertilisation, soil testing, improved nutrient-use efficiency and appropriate integration of organic and organomineral nutrient sources can reduce unnecessary dependence on purchased chemical fertiliser while maintaining productivity.

From Stock Management to Farmer Security

The current crisis should therefore be viewed as a warning rather than merely a temporary distribution problem.

Bangladesh may have sufficient fertiliser at the national level and still experience a fertiliser crisis at the farmer level. Both statements can be true at the same time.

The real test of a food-security system is not what appears in a national warehouse report. It is what happens when a farmer goes to the authorised dealer during the critical fertilisation window.

The real test — five questions at the dealer counter

  • Can the farmer obtain the correct product?
  • Is it available in the required quantity?
  • Is it sold at the official price?
  • Is the dealer accountable?
  • Can the government verify the movement of that fertiliser?

These questions are more important than any single national stock figure.

Bangladesh has already demonstrated its ability to procure and distribute fertiliser on a very large scale. The next challenge is to make that system more transparent, responsive and resilient.

The immediate disruption should be resolved quickly, but the larger opportunity should not be missed. Instead of repeatedly responding to fertiliser crises after they appear in the market, Bangladesh should build a system capable of detecting shortages before they reach farmers.

The bottom line

“The ultimate measure of fertiliser security is not how much fertiliser Bangladesh has in its warehouses. It is whether a farmer can obtain the right fertiliser, at the right price, at the right place and at the right time.”
